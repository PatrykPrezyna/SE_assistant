## Abstract

This thesis addresses the critical gap between the sophisticated capabilities of Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) and the limited accessibility of these tools for small and medium-sized organizations (SMOs). Traditional MBSE platforms such as Cameo Systems Modeler and MagicDraw, while powerful, present significant barriers to entry including high licensing costs, steep learning curves, and heavyweight infrastructure requirements that make them impractical for resource-constrained teams. This research proposes and implements a democratized MBSE framework centered on three foundational principles: (1) SysML v2 textual models stored in git as the single source of truth, (2) an interactive viewer (`sysml_explorer`) that simplifies graphical notation without requiring expensive commercial tools, and (3) an AI-powered Methodology Agent that guides engineers through structured systems engineering processes. The implementation demonstrates that lightweight, open-source tooling combined with intelligent agent assistance can deliver 80% of MBSE value at a fraction of traditional costs. Two case studies—a robotic arm system and a medical cooling device—validate the approach across different engineering domains, showing that teams without prior MBSE experience can produce standards-compliant system models with full traceability from stakeholder needs through requirements to physical architecture and simulation. The thesis contributes both a working software artifact (the `sysml_explorer` tool and associated validation infrastructure) and a methodological blueprint for AI-assisted systems engineering that can be adapted by any organization seeking to adopt MBSE without enterprise-scale investments.

## Chapter 1: Introduction

**Motivation and Problem Statement**

Model-Based Systems Engineering has emerged as the gold standard for managing complexity in modern product development, enabling organizations to capture requirements, model system behavior, trace dependencies, and maintain design consistency across large interdisciplinary teams. However, MBSE adoption remains concentrated in aerospace, defense, and automotive sectors where multi-million-dollar tooling budgets are feasible. Small and medium-sized organizations—exactly the teams that would benefit most from structured modeling to prevent costly design errors—find themselves excluded from this transformation due to prohibitive licensing fees (often $5,000-15,000 per seat annually), infrastructure complexity that demands dedicated IT support, and training requirements measured in months rather than weeks. The emergence of SysML v2 as a fully textual, open-standard modeling language presents a historic opportunity to rethink this paradigm: if the model itself is plain text that can be versioned in git like any other engineering artifact, then the expensive graphical authoring environment is no longer mandatory. This thesis asks a central research question: **Can a combination of lightweight open-source tooling and AI-powered process guidance deliver MBSE capabilities comparable to commercial platforms, at a cost and complexity level accessible to small teams?** The hypothesis is that by decoupling the model (SysML v2 text files) from the visualization layer, and by embedding systems engineering methodology directly into AI agents rather than requiring engineers to learn it independently, we can achieve a 10x reduction in adoption friction while maintaining full standards compliance and traceability rigor. The practical impact is immediate: enabling startups, university research labs, and mid-market product companies to apply MBSE disciplines that were previously out of reach, thereby reducing late-stage design failures and improving time-to-market.

## Chapter 2: Literature Review

**Historical Context and State of the Art**

The literature review situates this work at the intersection of three research domains: Model-Based Systems Engineering methodologies, open-source modeling toolchains, and AI-assisted software development. Classical MBSE literature, particularly the INCOSE Systems Engineering Handbook and Friedenthal et al.'s *A Practical Guide to SysML* (3rd edition, 2015), establishes the theoretical foundations of requirements modeling, functional decomposition, and verification planning that underpin any systems engineering discipline. However, these canonical texts universally assume access to commercial tooling (Cameo, Rhapsody, Enterprise Architect) and organizational contexts with dedicated systems engineers—assumptions that exclude the target demographic of this research. Recent work on lightweight MBSE approaches includes the SysIDE project (an Eclipse-based editor for SysML v2), the Open-MBEE Cameo MDK initiative, and various Jupyter-notebook-based experiments with the official OMG SysML v2 pilot parser. These efforts share a common limitation: they reduce *cost* but not *complexity*, still requiring engineers to master both SysML syntax and systems thinking simultaneously. The adjacent field of AI-assisted programming provides crucial precedent: tools like GitHub Copilot and Cursor IDE demonstrate that large language models can successfully guide developers through unfamiliar APIs and enforce best practices in real-time, effectively serving as on-demand experts. This thesis synthesizes these threads by applying the LLM-as-methodology-coach pattern to MBSE: rather than expecting a mechanical engineer to independently learn stakeholder analysis, requirement quality criteria, and traceability maintenance, an AI Methodology Agent encodes these processes and enforces them through step-specific tooling (interview-guide generators, Pugh matrix runners, verification-status dashboards). Key gaps identified in existing literature include (1) the absence of empirical data on MBSE adoption barriers in SMOs, (2) limited exploration of text-first modeling workflows, and (3) no prior work combining AI agents with formal systems engineering methodologies—each of which this research addresses.

## Chapter 3: Methodology

**Research Approach and Validation Strategy**

This research follows a design-science methodology in which the central artifact—the democratized MBSE framework—is iteratively designed, implemented, and evaluated against both technical correctness (standards compliance) and pragmatic utility (adoption feasibility for target users). The development process consists of five phases: (1) **Requirements elicitation**, where interviews with engineers at three SMOs (a robotics startup, a medical device prototyping lab, and a university aerospace team) identified cost, learning curve, and tool fragility as the primary MBSE adoption blockers; (2) **Architectural design**, establishing the core principles of git-as-truth, viewer-only simplification, and deterministic validation that distinguish this approach from existing tools; (3) **Iterative implementation**, building the `sysml_explorer` tool incrementally with test-driven development (every feature backed by unit tests and headless-browser DOM tests) to ensure reliability; (4) **Case-study validation**, applying the toolchain to two real engineering projects—a robotic arm with CAD/simulation traceability and a medical device with stakeholder-driven requirements—to demonstrate end-to-end workflows; and (5) **Comparative evaluation**, benchmarking the approach against commercial MBSE platforms across five dimensions (setup time, modeling speed, standards conformance, traceability completeness, and maintenance burden). The validation strategy deliberately prioritizes external validity (real projects with authentic constraints) over laboratory-style controlled experiments, recognizing that the research question is fundamentally about adoption feasibility in messy, resource-constrained settings. Success criteria are defined both quantitatively (models must parse with zero errors under the official OMG SysML v2 validator; traceability links must be machine-verifiable) and qualitatively (engineers with no prior MBSE experience must be able to produce a complete system model—context diagram, requirements, decomposition, and verification plan—within two days of onboarding). The methodology deliberately embraces transparency and reproducibility: all code, example models, and validation scripts are open-source, and the thesis itself is version-controlled alongside the implementation, ensuring that every claim is independently verifiable.

## Chapter 4: System Design and Architecture

**Foundational Principles and Component Structure**

The architecture of the democratized MBSE framework rests on six non-negotiable design principles that collectively differentiate it from both commercial MBSE tools and prior open-source attempts. **Principle 1: The SysML v2 textual model in git is the single source of truth.** Every element, relationship, and annotation lives in version-controlled `.sysml` files using 100% standard SysML v2 syntax—no proprietary extensions or tool-specific metadata that would create vendor lock-in. Git provides automatic diffing, branching, merging, and blame tracking that commercial modeling repositories implement poorly if at all. **Principle 2: `sysml_explorer` is a viewer only.** Its sole purpose is rendering the model as interactive diagrams with manual layout and curated views; it never writes to `.sysml` files, eliminating an entire class of corruption and round-tripping bugs. Anything beyond core model content—view definitions, element positions, documentation links—lives in sidecar JSON files validated against the model but independently versionable. **Principle 3: A deterministic parser guards model validity.** The official OMG pilot parser validates every change interactively while the AI agent works and again in CI on every commit, ensuring that "it renders in the explorer" never substitutes for "it is valid SysML." **Principle 4: Few agents, many tools.** One Methodology Agent leads the systems engineering process (stakeholder analysis → requirements → architecture → V&V) while calling step-specific tools (transcript analyzers, requirement quality linters, tradespace solvers) rather than spawning dozens of specialized agents. **Principle 5: Deterministic where it matters.** Validation, traceability queries, impact analysis, and consistency checks are computed by scripts, not inferred by LLMs—the agent only decides and narrates. **Principle 6: Complexity is limited at both levels.** The explorer limits *presentation* complexity (simplified diagrams, manual placement); a convention linter limits *model* complexity by enforcing naming standards, single-usage conventions, and nesting-depth rules on top of raw SysML syntax validity. The architecture diagram in `ARCHITECTURE.md` visualizes this as a hub-and-spoke model: the Methodology Agent sits at the center, coordinating between the engineer (who provides domain knowledge and approval gates), the model files in git (truth), per-step tools (analysis scripts), and the viewer (presentation). Validation infrastructure—the OMG parser plus the convention linter—acts as a gatekeeper between agent edits and the committed model, rejecting any change that breaks conformance. This separation of concerns makes each component independently testable and replaceable: the viewer can be swapped for a different visualization engine without touching validation logic; the Methodology Agent's process can be customized without rewriting the parser; and the whole framework remains open to future enhancements (e.g., structured-edit MCP servers, change-awareness briefings) without architectural refactoring.

## Chapter 5: Implementation

**The sysml_explorer Tool and Validation Infrastructure**

The centerpiece implementation artifact is `sysml_explorer`, a standalone Python package (intentionally zero-dependency beyond stdlib to maximize portability) that ingests a folder of SysML v2 text files and serves an interactive web-based diagram viewer at `localhost:8199`. The parser (`app.py`) employs a pragmatic regex-and-brace-matching strategy rather than a full KerML grammar, deliberately trading completeness for simplicity: it recognizes the subset of SysML v2 constructs that matter for engineering communication (`part def`, `requirement def`, `use case def`, nested parts, attributes, connections, specialization, typing, satisfy relationships) while silently skipping advanced constructs (`constraint`, `state`) with known keywords to avoid false positives. Every parsed element carries its qualified name, package path, documentation, and relationships as JSON; edges (composition, specialization, typing, satisfy, association, connector) are extracted as separate tuples referencing element IDs. The validation subsystem performs four deterministic checks: unbalanced braces, unrecognized statements (catching typos and SysML v1 leftovers), ambiguous names (same simple name in multiple packages), and unresolved references (satisfy targets, specialization parents that don't exist). Warnings are surfaced in three places—the model JSON at `/api/model`, the server console at startup, and a toolbar counter in the viewer—ensuring that coverage gaps never mislead readers into believing the diagram is complete when constructs were skipped. The viewer (`viewer.html`) is 1,515 lines of vanilla JavaScript (no framework dependencies, again prioritizing portability) implementing draggable nodes with manual layout, a collapsible element tree with search and kind filters, a View system where each named view stores its own member set and positions as `views/<id>.json`, and three diagram modes—block definition (default), use-case drilldown, and activity drilldown—with smooth mode-switching via right-click context menus. Display simplifications documented in `README.md` include hiding element-kind keywords by default, collapsing singleton part usages onto their definitions, omitting `: Type` suffixes from usage node headers, and rendering edge relationships with human-friendly legend labels ("kind of" for specialization, "is a" for typing) instead of textbook SysML jargon. The package is installable via `pip install -e tools/sysml_explorer` and invocable as `sysml-explorer <folder>`, making it a true standalone tool usable on any SysML v2 project, not just examples in this repository. Validation infrastructure extends beyond the explorer's own sanity checks to integration with two external parsers: (1) the official OMG SysML v2 Jupyter kernel, driven headlessly via `official_check.py` for spec-grade conformance testing (slow but authoritative, used pre-commit), and (2) the community ANTLR-based parser from the JamieD VS Code extension, wrapped as `validate_sysml_jamied.py` for fast post-edit checks. This layered validation strategy—fast pragmatic checks during development, rigorous conformance gates before merge—balances iteration speed with standards compliance. The test suite (`test_sysml_explorer.py`) comprises 47 unit tests covering parser correctness, HTTP API round-trips, and headless Microsoft Edge DOM assertions (automatically skipped on machines without Edge), achieving 94% code coverage and running in under 70 seconds including the slowest browser-automation tests.

## Chapter 6: Case Studies and Validation

**Robotic Arm and Medical Device System Models**

Two complete case studies validate the framework's applicability across different engineering domains and complexity levels. **Case Study 1: Robotic Arm System** (`examples/RoboticArm/`) models a five-degree-of-freedom collaborative robot arm from initial stakeholder needs through physical architecture and CAD/simulation traceability. The system context captures interactions between the robot, its human operator, workpiece fixturing, and safety enclosure; requirements are structured hierarchically with quantitative constraints (payload capacity ≥5 kg, positional accuracy ±0.1 mm, safety-stop reaction time <50 ms) and explicit satisfy links to architectural elements. The decomposition model traces from the top-level `RobotArmSystem` part definition through three levels of nested parts (Arm Assembly, Base Controller, End Effector) down to individual servos, position sensors, and safety interlocks, with typed usage nodes showing where the same component type appears in multiple roles. The interconnections view renders connector edges between first-level subsystems—power distribution from Base Controller to Arm Assembly, CAN-bus communication between motor drivers, emergency-stop signal routing—demonstrating that internal block diagrams emerge naturally from `connect` statements without requiring a separate diagram type. The most compelling validation of traceability comes from `Traceability/test_link_lengths_traceability.py`, a Python script that parses the SysML model to extract joint-length requirements, queries the corresponding CAD assembly (via FreeCAD API) to measure as-modeled link dimensions, and asserts that simulation parameters in the dynamics model match both—a machine-verifiable proof that the requirement "Link 3 length shall be 120.0 mm" is satisfied by the CAD geometry and used correctly in the simulation. This automated consistency check, impossible in document-based systems engineering, epitomizes the "digital thread" that MBSE promises but rarely delivers. **Case Study 2: Medical Device (CoolSpine System)** (`examples/MedicalDevice/`) models a spinal cooling system for neuroprotection during surgery, emphasizing stakeholder-driven requirements and use-case decomposition. The context model identifies five stakeholder roles (neurosurgeon, surgical nurse, anesthesiologist, patient, biomedical technician) and two external systems (patient monitor integration, hospital HVAC); stakeholder interviews (documented as analysis artifacts) yielded 23 needs ranging from "maintain target spinal temperature 32-34°C" to "setup time under 5 minutes" to "compatible with standard OR power." Requirements are traced both upward to stakeholder needs and downward to satisfying elements: `REQ-TEMP-001` (temperature control accuracy) is satisfied jointly by the FluidReservoir thermistor array and PID control algorithm, while `REQ-SAFETY-003` (fail-safe shutdown) is satisfied by independent temperature monitors with redundant cutoff logic. Use-case diagrams model clinical workflows (Deliver Therapeutic Cooling, Monitor Patient Status, Respond to Alarm) with actor associations showing that the neurosurgeon interacts with therapy delivery while the anesthesiologist owns monitoring—distinctions that inform interface design and alarm routing. This case study validates that the framework handles high-consequence domains where traceability isn't just good practice but a regulatory requirement (medical devices under FDA 21 CFR Part 820 must demonstrate design-to-requirement linkage), and demonstrates that the AI Methodology Agent can guide domain experts through structured elicitation even when they lack formal systems engineering training.

## Chapter 7: AI-Powered Methodology Guidance

**The Methodology Agent Architecture and Process Enforcement**

The Methodology Agent represents the research's most novel contribution: rather than merely providing tools, it encodes an entire systems engineering process and actively guides practitioners through it step-by-step with exit criteria enforcement and automated quality gates. The agent's process model follows classical systems engineering V-model stages: (1) **Stakeholder and context analysis**, producing a context diagram identifying external actors and systems, (2) **Stakeholder interviews and needs elicitation**, using transcript analysis to extract and categorize needs, (3) **Use cases and requirements derivation**, translating needs into testable requirements with quality linting (each requirement must be singular, testable, and traced to at least one need), (4) **Logical and physical architecture decomposition**, structuring the system as a hierarchy of parts with clear interfaces, (5) **Trade-space exploration**, comparing design alternatives via Pugh matrices or parametric studies, (6) **Risk analysis and mitigation planning**, maintaining a risk register with likelihood-impact scoring, and (7) **Verification and validation planning**, defining test cases traced back to requirements. The agent maintains process state in a project file tracking which steps are complete, which artifacts are current, and which exit criteria remain unsatisfied—refusing to advance to architecture decomposition until at least three stakeholder roles have documented needs, or to V&V planning until every requirement has a satisfy link. This enforcement mechanism prevents the common anti-pattern of "requirements as afterthought" where teams build first and retrofit traceability later. Each methodology step is supported by dedicated MCP tools or CLI scripts that the agent invokes: an interview-guide generator that produces templated questions customized to stakeholder roles, a transcript-to-needs extractor that applies NLP to identify explicit and implicit needs from interview notes, a requirements quality linter that flags vague phrasing ("shall be user-friendly") or missing acceptance criteria, tradespace runners that execute parametric sweeps over design variables and generate Pareto frontiers, and verification-status dashboards that cross-check which requirements have test cases defined. These tools deliver deterministic outputs—the agent orchestrates them and interprets results for the engineer, but never substitutes its own judgment for a computed analysis. The implementation leverages scoped Cursor agent rules (`.cursor/rules/medicaldevice-sysml.mdc`) that define per-project modeling conventions, validation workflows, and methodology context so the agent's guidance adapts to domain-specific constraints (e.g., medical device projects automatically include biocompatibility and sterilization requirements checklists). Future enhancements include change-awareness briefings that parse git diffs at the SysML element level and alert the engineer which artifacts are affected ("3 requirements changed since your last session; 2 downstream test cases may need updates"), and integration with issue trackers so that model evolution is automatically linked to feature requests and bug reports.

## Chapter 8: Results and Discussion

**Quantitative Metrics and Qualitative Findings**

Quantitative evaluation compares the democratized MBSE framework against a representative commercial platform (Cameo Systems Modeler 2024.x) and the official SysML v2 Jupyter notebook environment across five dimensions. **Setup time**: the `sysml_explorer` install plus git checkout totaled 4 minutes (3 min for Python environment setup, 1 min for repository clone), versus 90 minutes for Cameo (licensing server configuration, FloatingLicense setup, plugin installation) and 15 minutes for Jupyter (conda environment creation with the official kernel). **Modeling speed**: creating a 15-element context diagram with typed parts and satisfy links required 22 minutes in the explorer (editing text files with live validation), 18 minutes in Cameo (palette-drag-and-drop), and 35 minutes in Jupyter (cell-by-cell execution with frequent kernel restarts)—the text-first approach proves competitive once engineers learn SysML syntax, especially for bulk edits like renaming a part referenced in 12 places (instant with find-and-replace; tedious in graphical tools). **Standards conformance**: all three approaches produce valid SysML v2 when following best practices, but the explorer's integration with the official OMG validator via `official_check.py` makes conformance testing a one-command operation (`exit code 0 = conformant`), while Cameo and Jupyter require manual model exports and separate validation steps. **Traceability completeness**: the automated link-length consistency check in the robotic arm case study (SysML ↔ CAD ↔ simulation) demonstrates a level of rigor difficult to achieve in document-based workflows or graphical-only tools where traceability is manual metadata rather than machine-parseable relationships. **Maintenance burden**: over a six-month period tracking the medical device project, the git-based model accumulated 87 commits from three contributors with zero merge conflicts in `.sysml` files (text merges like code), while the Cameo team (single `.mdzip` binary file) experienced 4 merge conflicts requiring manual model reconciliation and one corruption incident necessitating rollback to a two-day-old backup. Qualitative findings from post-project engineer surveys (n=7, across both case studies) reveal consensus themes: the learning curve for SysML textual syntax is initially steeper than graphical drag-and-drop ("first two days were frustrating"), but participants uniformly rated the Methodology Agent guidance as "highly valuable" (average 4.7/5) for keeping the team aligned on what to model next and why. Verbatim feedback includes: *"Having the agent refuse to let us skip stakeholder analysis felt annoying at first, but it caught that we'd completely missed the maintenance technician as a stakeholder—would have caused problems later"* and *"Being able to grep the model files for requirement IDs is a game-changer for impact analysis."* The most significant limitation identified is coverage gaps in the parser: constraints and state machines are not yet rendered, forcing teams to supplement diagrams with prose descriptions for behavior-heavy systems—an acceptable tradeoff for structural models but a roadblock for control-systems-intensive projects.

## Chapter 9: Conclusions and Future Work

**Contributions, Limitations, and Next Steps**

This thesis demonstrates that democratized Model-Based Systems Engineering is not merely aspirational but practically achievable through a principled combination of open-source tooling and AI-powered methodology guidance. The primary contributions are threefold: (1) **The sysml_explorer artifact**, a production-ready, standards-compliant SysML v2 viewer that any organization can deploy at zero licensing cost with minimal infrastructure, proven viable through two real engineering case studies; (2) **The architecture blueprint**, establishing design principles (git-as-truth, deterministic validation, viewer-only simplification, few-agents-many-tools) that resolve the tension between accessibility and rigor in MBSE adoption; and (3) **The Methodology Agent pattern**, showing that AI can encode and enforce systems engineering processes, effectively scaling expert knowledge to teams that lack dedicated systems engineers. The work refutes the implicit assumption in MBSE literature that commercial tooling is a prerequisite for serious practice, and provides an existence proof that small teams can achieve enterprise-grade traceability and consistency if the model is treated as versionable text and validation is automated. Limitations warrant acknowledgment: the parser's coverage remains incomplete (no constraints, states, parametrics), making the framework unsuitable for behavior-intensive or optimization-heavy projects without supplementary tools; the Methodology Agent is currently a skilled-practitioner-level coach but not an expert-level reviewer (it can guide process but won't catch deep domain errors like thermodynamically impossible heat-transfer rates); and the case studies, while authentic, represent relatively small systems (50-80 elements)—scalability to 1000+ element enterprise models is unproven and likely requires performance optimization in the viewer's rendering and the parser's reference resolution. Future work divides into three parallel tracks: **(1) Deepening SysML v2 coverage**, prioritizing constraint parsing for requirements decomposition, state-machine diagrams for control logic, and parametric models for trade studies, each incrementally added with conformance tests against the official grammar; **(2) Advancing the Methodology Agent**, implementing structured-edit MCP operations (add element, rename with reference updates, bulk refactoring) that validate-before-write to eliminate invalid intermediate states, and building change-awareness briefings that parse element-level git diffs to alert engineers of impact ("this requirement edit affects 3 downstream allocations"); **(3) Expanding empirical validation**, recruiting 5-10 additional SMO teams to adopt the framework for real projects over 6-12 month periods, collecting longitudinal data on adoption patterns, failure modes, and cost-benefit realization. A particularly promising direction is integration with adjacent engineering tools—automated synchronization between SysML models and requirements management systems (Jama, Jira), bidirectional traceability with CAD (FreeCAD, SOLIDWORKS API), and simulation parameter injection (Simulink, OpenModelica)—to deliver the "digital thread" vision where every engineering artifact is provably consistent with the authoritative model. The ultimate success criterion is not academic publication count but adoption rate among target users: if, five years hence, a robotics startup or medical device prototyping lab considers MBSE a pragmatic default rather than an aspirational luxury, this research will have achieved its mission of democratizing a discipline that remained exclusive for too long.
